## 1 · TL;DR (30-second read)

You're handed a **4-row** inverter-event export and asked: *does anything smell wrong enough to escalate?*

The honest read: **No — nothing in this file warrants escalation.** All four events have **`impact_mw = 0`**, are **`cleared = yes`**, are **`info`/`warning`** severity, last **≤ 7 minutes**, and hit **four different inverters** with no recurrence. The event *names* sound scary (`COMM_LINK_WARN`, `FAN_SPEED_HIGH`, `DC_INPUT_CHECK`) — but the exam explicitly tells you **not to infer failure from wording**, and the columns that measure impact all say zero.

**Defensible verdict → *Do not escalate.*** The only genuinely defensible "worth a look" is **completeness**: 4 events across 9 days is thin, so name the *cheap* check (is this the full, unfiltered export?) — without assuming that data is available. This scores well because it is **evidence-based** and **resists the wording trap**.

## 2 · Scenario in plain English

You are reviewing a **small inverter-event export** *before* a broader analysis session. The task: identify **at most two** things worth checking, explain why you would (or would not) spend more time on each, and reach a **scoped** conclusion — **escalate, do one cheap check, or stop.** You are explicitly allowed to conclude **nothing warrants escalation** — as long as the conclusion is **evidence-based**.

Why it matters: this is a **discipline test.** The names look alarming; the data does not. Over-escalating benign telemetry wastes the plant team's time and trains people to ignore alerts; under-checking a truly truncated export misses real problems. The skill is calibrating to **what the columns actually show**, and naming the *smallest* useful next step.

## 5 · Verified data findings (the frozen analysis — cite these)

**1. Four events, four different inverters, no recurrence.**
The export has exactly **4 rows** spanning **27 May → 4 Jun** (9 calendar days), on **INV-17, INV-03, INV-22, INV-09** — each inverter appears **once**. No inverter shows a repeating or worsening pattern. *(source: `inverter_events.csv`)*

**2. Every event is zero-impact and already cleared.**
All 4 rows have **`impact_mw = 0`** and **`cleared = yes`**. Severity is **`info`** for three and **`warning`** for one (`COMM_LINK_WARN`); none is `error`/`critical`. Durations are **1–7 minutes**. *(source: `inverter_events.csv`)*

**3. The event names are routine telemetry, not fault reports.**
`COMM_LINK_WARN` (a comms link warning), `FAN_SPEED_HIGH` (info), `DC_INPUT_CHECK` (a check), `TEMP_SENSOR_RECOVERED` (explicitly *recovered*) — none asserts a generation fault, and the impact/severity/cleared columns corroborate "benign." *(source: `inverter_events.csv`)*

**4. The only real question is completeness — and it isn't answerable from this file.**
4 events across 9 days is **sparse** for a multi-inverter plant. Nothing in the file tells you whether it is the **full** export or a **filtered/truncated** slice (no total-count, no plant-wide baseline). That is the single thing worth a *cheap* check — but the exam warns you **not to assume** another source is available. *(source: `inverter_events.csv` — absence of scope metadata)*

## 7 · Traps that lose marks here

> [!CAUTION]
> - **Inferring failure from event names.** The rubric bans this explicitly. `FAN_SPEED_HIGH`/`DC_INPUT_CHECK` with `impact_mw = 0` and `cleared = yes` are not faults.
> - **Escalating anything.** There is no impact, no recurrence, no open event — escalation is unsupported.
> - **Padding to look thorough.** The box caps you at **five items** and 3000 chars; a tight, calibrated note beats a long anxious one.
> - **Assuming the missing data exists.** You may *name* the completeness check, but don't assume the full export/baseline is available.
> - **Ignoring completeness entirely.** The opposite error — 4 rows over 9 days *is* thin; a good answer notes it as a **cheap check**, not an alarm.
> - **Over-reading the lone `warning`.** One `COMM_LINK_WARN`, cleared, zero impact, is still benign.
